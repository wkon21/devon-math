Week 1 · Lesson A

Early Man: Competition, Cooperation & Altruism

Was human evolution solely a violent struggle for survival, or was mutual aid our greatest evolutionary advantage? Prehistoric archaeology demonstrates that cooperation, empathy, and social care defined our lineage long before the dawn of agriculture.

🦴 Shanidar 1 ("Nandy")

Discovered in Iraqi Kurdistan, Shanidar 1 was a Neanderthal man who lived into his 40s (ancient for that era) despite suffering a crushed left eye socket (likely blind), a severed/withered right arm, and severe deformities in his leg and foot. In a harsh Ice Age environment, he could not hunt or forage alone. His survival proves his group actively fed, protected, and cared for disabled members.

🎨 Neanderthal Culture in France

Excavations at sites like Bruniquel Cave and La Chapelle-aux-Saints revealed stalagmite ring structures, seashell jewelry with mineral pigments, and intentional burial pits with floral tributes and grave goods. This confirms symbolic thinking, group identity, and rituals far beyond pure biological survival.

🤝 Group Solidarity & Altruism

While animals display kin selection (protecting genetic relatives), early humans developed reciprocal altruism and shared language. Groups with high trust and mutual food-sharing consistently out-survived hyper-competitive, selfish groups during climate fluctuations.

Week 2 · Lesson B

Kinship, Survival & Indigenous Case Studies

Before courts, police, and contracts existed, kinship was the total organizing framework of human life. It dictated who shared food, who resolved disputes, and who protected the group from outside threats.

🌲 Kinship vs. Family

While "family" usually refers to biological parents and children, kinship is a broader socio-cultural web of rights, reciprocal duties, and lineage. In many cultures, aunts are called "mother," and all clan members share equal responsibility for raising offspring and protecting communal hunting grounds.

🏞 Karen Tribes of Northern Thailand

The Karen people organize around matrilineal descent (property passes through female lines). Their traditional rotational farming ("swidden") is rooted in collective spiritual stewardship: land cannot be individually sold or privatized because it belongs to the collective ancestors and unborn descendants.

🏹 North Sentinel Island (Nov 2018)

The Sentinelese people of the Andaman Islands maintain total self-isolation. In November 2018, an American missionary was killed attempting to breach their perimeter. From an anthropological lens, their fierce hostility is an extreme defense mechanism: having zero acquired immunity to modern pathogens, contact would cause immediate demographic collapse. Kinship boundaries equal survival.

Week 3 · Lesson C

Kinship, Scale & Fairness: Émile Durkheim & Maslow

What happens when groups grow from 50 hunter-gatherers to millions in an industrial city? Sociologist Émile Durkheim examined how the bonds that hold societies together fundamentally change as scale expands.

⚙️ Mechanical vs. Organic Solidarity

Mechanical Solidarity (Small/Kinship): Individuals perform identical tasks, share identical religious beliefs, and adhere to strict conformity. Solidarity arises from likeness.
Organic Solidarity (Modern/Complex): High division of labor (doctors, plumbers, coders, farmers). Individuals are radically different, yet bound together by mutual interdependence.

📊 Dunbar's Number (~150)

Evolutionary psychologist Robin Dunbar identified that human brain neocortex volume limits genuine, stable social relationships to approximately 150 people. Beyond 150, kinship and peer-pressure can no longer govern behavior—societies require formal institutions: police, written laws, money, and taxation.

🔺 Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

Societies can be evaluated by how effectively their economic system fulfills the hierarchy: (1) Physiological (food/water), (2) Safety (shelter/healthcare), (3) Belonging (community/kin), (4) Esteem (respect/purpose), (5) Self-Actualization. When economic systems fail basic needs, social solidarity fractures.

Week 4 · Lesson D

Socioeconomic Systems: Replacing Kinship

As societies outgrew kinship, they developed formal socioeconomic systems to organize production, distribute resources, and manage labor: Slavery → Feudalism → Capitalism → Socialism.

🏭 Capitalism

Ownership: Private individuals and corporations.
Allocation: Market supply and demand; price mechanisms.
Incentive: Profit motive and capital accumulation.
Pros/Cons: Unmatched innovation and productivity; high economic inequality and risk of market failure.

🏛 Socialism & Social Democracy

Ownership: Collective/public ownership of critical utilities (healthcare, transit, energy) alongside private enterprise.
Allocation: Progressive taxation funding universal public goods.
Goal: High floor of human dignity and low poverty (e.g. Nordic Model).

🚩 Communism (Theoretical vs State)

Marxist Ideal: A classless, stateless, moneyless collective where resources are shared: "From each according to ability, to each according to need."
Historical Practice: Highly centralized state-command economies with bureaucratic rationing and restricted civil liberties.

Week 5 · Lesson E

Equality vs. Equity: Bregman's "Utopia for Realists"

Is "fairness" giving everyone the exact same thing (Equality), or giving everyone what they need to have equal access and dignity (Equity)? Historian Rutger Bregman argues that abolishing poverty is not an impossible dream, but an affordable investment.

⚖️ Equality vs. Equity

Equality: Providing everyone with identical resources regardless of starting position (giving a size-10 shoe to everyone).
Equity: Providing customized resources to eliminate systemic barriers (giving everyone shoes that fit their specific feet).

📖 Rutger Bregman's Core Thesis

Poverty is not a personality defect or lack of work ethic; poverty is simply a lack of cash. The cognitive load of constant financial scarcity drops effective IQ by 13 points (the equivalent of an entire sleepless night). Provide direct cash, and health, education, and child outcomes instantly surge.

🌾 The Dauphin "Mincome" Experiment

In Manitoba, Canada (1974–1979), every resident was guaranteed a basic income floor. Results: Hospitalizations dropped by 8.5%, high school graduation rates jumped, and only two groups reduced hours: mothers of newborns and teenagers staying in school longer.

Weeks 6 & 7 · Lesson F

Applied Equity: Automation & Universal Basic Income

As generative AI, software agents, and robotics accelerate, does every human still need to work 40 hours a week to survive? Universal Basic Income proposes decoupling basic survival from traditional wage labor.

🤖 The Automation Dilemma

Past industrial revolutions replaced manual physical labor (horses replaced by tractors). The AI revolution replaces cognitive and analytical tasks (coding, accounting, writing). If productivity skyrockets while labor demand contracts, who captures the wealth?

💡 Three Pillars of UBI

1. Universal: Given to every citizen regardless of income or net worth.
2. Unconditional: No means-testing, no job-seeking mandates, no drug tests.
3. Individual: Paid to each adult directly, not per household.

🪤 Smashing the "Poverty Trap"

Under traditional welfare, earning $1 causes $0.80 of benefits to be withdrawn (an effective 80% marginal tax rate on the poor!). UBI is never clawed back when you work, meaning every extra dollar earned strictly improves your situation.

Weeks 8 & 9 · Lesson G

Primary Sources & Criterion C/D Writing Lab

Master the primary and secondary evidence for the UBI Summative Essay: Keynes' predictions, the Helsinki Times experiment data, and how to structure a Criterion C/D op-ed.

📜 J.M. Keynes (1930)

In Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, British economist Keynes predicted that by 2030, technological efficiency would solve "the economic problem," resulting in a 15-hour workweek where humans focus on arts, leisure, and philosophy.

🇫🇮 The Finnish Experiment (Helsinki Times)

Finland gave 2,000 unemployed citizens €560/month unconditionally. Evaluators found recipients experienced significantly lower stress, fewer symptoms of depression, higher institutional trust, and slightly higher employment rates than the control group.

✍️ Criterion C & D Essay Structure

Hook & Thesis: Define equity and state your policy stance.
Argument 1 (Security): Evidence on poverty reduction & health.
Argument 2 (Pre-empt Counter): Address affordability & inflation.
Conclusion: Synthesize how cooperation builds a sustainable future.
