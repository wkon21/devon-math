Week 1 · Lesson A

Early Man: Competition, Cooperation & Altruism

Was early human evolution a vicious, Hobbesian "war of all against all", or was empathy and cooperation our greatest evolutionary weapon? Anthropology and archaeological finds show that long before agriculture, our hominin ancestors developed profound systems of mutual aid, disability care, and symbolic culture.

🦴 Shanidar 1 ("Nandy") — Disability in the Paleolithic

Excavated in the Zagros Mountains of Iraqi Kurdistan, the Neanderthal fossil known as Shanidar 1 dates to ~45,000 BP. He survived until age 40–50 despite horrific physical trauma suffered in his youth:

  • Crushed left eye socket causing total or partial blindness.
  • Withered, amputated right arm broken and healed at the elbow.
  • Severe right leg trauma and deformed gait leading to extreme mobility impairment.

In a harsh periglacial environment, he could neither chase big game nor forage efficiently alone. His survival proves his community actively transported, fed, defended, and valued him for decades.

🎨 Neanderthal Symbolic Culture in France

Discoveries across French archaeological sites dismantled the Victorian myth of Neanderthals as mindless beasts:

  • Bruniquel Cave (176,500 BP): Deep inside total darkness, Neanderthals intentionally snapped hundreds of stalagmites into rings and arranged them around deliberate fire pits.
  • La Chapelle-aux-Saints & La Ferrassie: Deliberate rectangular burial pits dug into bedrock, with skeletons positioned in sleeping poses accompanied by stone tools and animal bones (grave goods).
  • Body Adornment: Perforated marine shells stained with red ochre and black manganese dioxide pigments worn as necklaces.

🤝 Darwin vs. Kropotkin: The Evolution of Altruism

While 19th-century Social Darwinists weaponized "survival of the fittest" to justify ruthless greed, evolutionary biologist Peter Kropotkin (Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution, 1902) proved that species that practice mutual support thrive far better than solitary combatants:

  • Kin Selection: Sacrificing for genetic relatives (Hamilton's Rule).
  • Reciprocal Altruism (Robert Trivers): Cooperating with non-relatives under the social expectation that assistance will be reciprocated in times of famine or injury.
  • Group Selection: Cohesive, altruistic bands easily out-survived fractured, selfish bands during glacial climate collapses.
Evolutionary Continuum: How mutual aid and compassion preserved human knowledge across ice ages.

Kinship, Survival & Indigenous Case Studies

Before courts, police forces, national borders, or written constitutions existed, kinship was the comprehensive operating system of human society. It organized production, governed territorial rights, dictated marriage alliances, and maintained order.

🌲 Kinship Systems vs. Nuclear Family

In modern industrialized nations, the "nuclear family" (parents + children) is often treated as an isolated economic unit. In indigenous and traditional societies, kinship is an expansive matrix:

  • Patrilineal: Descent and inheritance traced through fathers (e.g. ancient Rome, traditional Han lineage).
  • Matrilineal: Property, names, and clan leadership trace through mothers (e.g. Karen tribes, Iroquois Confederacy, Minangkabau of Indonesia).
  • Bilateral: Equal recognition of maternal and paternal lines (typical of mobile hunter-gatherers).

🏞 Karen Tribes of Northern Thailand: Communal Stewardship

The Karen (Pwo and Sgaw) inhabit the mountainous borders of Thailand and Myanmar. Their society offers a living model of non-market resource governance:

  • Swidden Agriculture (Hku): Rotational farming where fields are cleared, cultivated for one year, and fallowed for 7–10 years to allow rainforest regeneration.
  • Spiritual Tenure: Land cannot be sold for private profit; it belongs to the forest spirit (Thi Kho), collective ancestors, and future generations.
  • Matrilocal Residence: Husbands move into their bride's clan village, ensuring women maintain stable collective control over residential homes and food storage.

🏹 North Sentinel Island: The Sovereign Quarantine (2018)

The Sentinelese are an uncontacted hunter-gatherer tribe living on North Sentinel Island in the Bay of Bengal for ~60,000 years:

  • The Incident (Nov 2018): John Allen Chau, an American missionary, bribed local fishermen to illegally trespass onto the island to preach Christianity. He was killed by Sentinelese archers.
  • Anthropological Reality: The Sentinelese have zero immunities to common viral pathogens (influenza, measles, rhinovirus). Contact would cause catastrophic biological extinction, identical to what annihilated 95% of indigenous Americans post-1492.
  • Legal Sovereignty: The Indian government enforces an absolute exclusion zone under the Protection of Aboriginal Tribes Regulation 1956. The Sentinelese arrow is not malice; it is a defensive boundary maintaining their existence.
Primary Anthropological Fieldwork — Survival International
"The Sentinelese are fiercely independent and survive entirely on hunting, fishing, and gathering wild plants. Far from living in wretched squalor, they appear healthy, flourishing, and remarkably self-sufficient. Their hostility to outside incursions is entirely rational: every historical contact with neighboring Great Andamanese tribes resulted in violence, kidnapping, addiction, and near-total demographic extinction from foreign epidemics."
— Dr. Sophie Grig, Senior Researcher, Survival International (November 2018)

Week 3 · Lesson C

Kinship, Scale & Fairness: Émile Durkheim & Maslow

What happens when human settlements grow from 50 hunter-gatherers to cities of 10 million people? Sociologist Émile Durkheim demonstrated that the nature of social cohesion fundamentally transforms as societies expand in size, density, and specialization.

⚙️ Mechanical vs. Organic Solidarity (Durkheim, 1893)

In The Division of Labour in Society, Durkheim identified two distinct forms of social glue:

  • Mechanical Solidarity (Traditional / Kin-based): People perform identical tasks (all hunt, all gather). Cohesion is based on similarity and a powerful collective conscience (shared religion, strict taboos). Deviance is met with repressive law (harsh physical punishment).
  • Organic Solidarity (Modern / Industrial): Complex division of labor (specialized surgeons, plumbers, farmers, coders). Cohesion is based on interdependence—like organs in a biological body, no one can survive alone. Deviance is met with restitutive law (restoring contracts and societal balance).

🧠 Dunbar's Number (~150) & The Institutional Shift

Evolutionary psychologist Robin Dunbar established that primate brain neocortex size correlates with group size:

  • The 150 Ceiling: Humans can maintain stable, trusting personal relationships with at most ~150 individuals. In bands under 150, social gossip, peer pressure, and direct kinship naturally suppress cheating and unfairness.
  • The State Emerges: Beyond 150, anonymity enters. You cannot personally know or trust everyone. To prevent systemic breakdown, societies are forced to invent abstract institutions: police forces, written commercial laws, courts, currency, and taxation.

🔺 Maslow's Hierarchy & Structural Fairness

Psychologist Abraham Maslow (1943) mapped human motivation across five tiered levels:

  • 1. Physiological: Food, clean water, rest, warmth.
  • 2. Safety: Shelter, health security, protection from violence.
  • 3. Belonging & Love: Kinship, community, friendship, social connection.
  • 4. Esteem: Dignity, respect of peers, competence, agency.
  • 5. Self-Actualization: Realizing one's creative, intellectual, or personal potential.

Sociological Application: A society cannot be judged fair if it offers abstract freedom of speech while failing to guarantee Tier 1 and Tier 2 basic survival.

Week 4 · Lesson D

Socioeconomic Systems: Replacing Kinship

When human scale outgrew kinship, societies developed formalized political and economic systems to determine: What to produce? How to produce it? And who gets the output?

🏭 Capitalism (Free Market & Enterprise)

  • Key Thinker: Adam Smith (The Wealth of Nations, 1776).
  • Core Mechanisms: Private ownership of means of production, market pricing through supply and demand, competition, wage labor, and profit reinvestment.
  • Strengths: Massive technological innovation, rapid production scaling, consumer variety, capital efficiency.
  • Weaknesses & Market Failures: Monopolies, extreme wealth inequality, environmental externalities (carbon emissions treated as free), exploitation of unorganized labor.

🏛 Socialism & The Nordic Social Democratic Model

  • Core Mechanisms: Democratic governance where vital infrastructure (healthcare, education, water, public transit) is publicly owned or heavily regulated, while private business operates in consumer markets.
  • The Nordic Model (Denmark, Sweden, Norway): High progressive income taxes (up to 55%), free university education, universal healthcare, state pensions, and strong union collective bargaining ("flexicurity").
  • Goal: A robust social safety net ensuring no citizen falls below a civilized baseline of dignity regardless of market fortunes.

🚩 Communism: Theoretical Ideal vs. Soviet Reality

  • Marxist Theory (Karl Marx & Friedrich Engels): Historical materialism. Capitalism inevitably concentrates capital, prompting workers (proletariat) to seize the means of production, leading to a classless, stateless, moneyless collective: "From each according to ability, to each according to need."
  • Historical Practice (USSR, 1917–1991): Total state ownership, one-party political dictatorship, state planning committee (Gosplan) setting prices and quotas by decree. Led to bureaucratic paralysis, consumer goods shortages, loss of civil liberties, and economic stagnation.
Daily Life Comparison Across Three Economic Systems
1840s Manchester Factory Worker (Laissez-Faire Capitalism): 14-hour daily shifts six days a week, child chimney sweeps, zero sick pay, living in unventilated cellar tenements, life expectancy in industrial slums dropped below 20 years.

1970s Moscow Factory Worker (Soviet State Socialism): Guaranteed lifetime employment, free apartment (after a 7-year waitlist), free healthcare and education, but constant queues for basic shoes or meat, strict restrictions on speech and travel.

2020s Copenhagen Worker (Social Democracy): 37-hour workweek, 5 weeks paid mandatory vacation, 12 months paid parental leave, heavily subsidized high-quality childcare, unemployment insurance providing 90% salary replacement during retraining.
— Comparative Economic History Field Guide (Dwight MYP Humanities)

Week 5 · Lesson E

Equality vs. Equity: Bregman's "Utopia for Realists"

How should a just society distribute resources? Is fairness about identical inputs (Equality), or customized support to guarantee universal capability (Equity)? Dutch historian Rutger Bregman argues that eradicating poverty is not a utopian fantasy, but an economically sound, empirically tested imperative.

⚖️ Equality vs. Equity vs. Justice

Equality: Giving every individual the exact same resources regardless of need (e.g. giving every student a size-10 shoe, or allocating identical funding to an elite suburban school and an impoverished inner-city school).

Equity: Distributing resources proportionally based on individual barriers so that all have equal capability and real freedom (giving each person a shoe that fits their foot).

Justice: Dismantling the systemic barrier itself so that artificial support is no longer required.

🧠 The Cognitive Tax of Poverty (Mullainathan & Shafir)

In Utopia for Realists, Bregman highlights landmark research by Princeton and Harvard psychologists showing that poverty is fundamentally a scarcity of cash, not a defect of character:

  • Mental Bandwidth: Worrying about rent, overdue bills, and feeding children consumes enormous cognitive RAM.
  • 13-Point IQ Drop: Experimental tests on sugarcane farmers in India before harvest (when destitute) vs. after harvest (when paid) showed an instantaneous 13-point swing in fluid intelligence. Poverty behaves like staying awake for 24 continuous hours.

🇨🇦 The Dauphin "Mincome" Experiment (Manitoba, 1974–79)

For five years, the Canadian federal and provincial governments guaranteed a basic income floor to all 10,000 residents of the agricultural town of Dauphin, Manitoba:

  • Health Outcomes: Hospitalization rates plunged by 8.5%. Work-related accidents and domestic violence plummeted; mental health hospital visits dropped dramatically.
  • Education: High school completion rates reached all-time records as adolescents stayed in school rather than dropping out to work low-wage farm labor.
  • Work Hours: Primary breadwinners reduced hours by a negligible 1%. The only notable reductions occurred among new mothers caring for infants and young students completing high school.
Rutger Bregman — Utopia for Realists (Bloomsbury, 2016)
"Poverty is not a lack of character. It's a lack of cash. We treat poor people as if they are children who cannot be trusted with money, so we build massive bureaucratic apparatuses with caseworkers, forms, and punitive sanctions that cost billions of dollars to police. If you want to end poverty, the most efficient, cost-effective, and scientifically validated policy on Earth is stunningly simple: give people cash."
— Rutger Bregman, Chapter 2: 'Why We Should Give Free Money to Everyone'

Applied Equity: Automation, Work & Universal Basic Income

We are entering the Fourth Industrial Revolution. Machine learning, generative AI, algorithmic trading, and autonomous robotics are shifting labor from muscle displacement to brain displacement. Does every human still need to sell 40 hours of labor a week just to purchase the right to exist?

🤖 The Cognitive Automation Wave

During the 19th and 20th centuries, machines replaced physical muscle (tractors replaced oxen, assembly line cranes replaced dockworkers). Workers transitioned into cognitive, administrative, and service roles.

Generative AI and automated software agents displace routine cognitive labor: contract drafting, radiology screening, software coding, graphic generation, translation, and bookkeeping. When productivity expands while human labor hours contract, who reaps the economic surplus?

💡 The Three Non-Negotiable Pillars of UBI

  • 1. Universal: Paid to every legal adult citizen regardless of whether they are a barista, a schoolteacher, or a billionaire. (Wealthy citizens pay it back plus much more through progressive taxation).
  • 2. Unconditional: No means-testing, no mandatory job search logs, no drug tests, and no bureaucratic caseworker approvals. A true economic floor.
  • 3. Individual: Paid directly to each individual adult, ensuring economic autonomy for women and youth escaping domestic abuse.

🪤 Smashing the Traditional "Poverty Trap"

Under traditional means-tested welfare systems (e.g. food stamps, housing vouchers, income assistance):

  • The Welfare Cliff: If an unemployed person takes a part-time job paying $1,000/month, the state strips $800 of their welfare benefits and taxes them. Their effective marginal tax rate is 80%–90%! Rational people cannot afford to take a job.
  • The UBI Solution: UBI is never withdrawn when you find employment. If you earn $1,000, you keep your $1,000 UBI plus your job wages. Work always pays.
David Graeber — Bullshit Jobs: A Theory (Simon & Schuster, 2018)
"Huge swathes of people, in Europe and North America in particular, spend their entire working lives performing tasks they secretly believe do not really need to be performed. The moral and spiritual damage that comes from this situation is profound. It is a scar across our collective soul. UBI breaks this cycle by giving workers the unconditional power to walk away from pointless or toxic employment."
— David Graeber, Professor of Anthropology, London School of Economics

Primary & Secondary Source Evaluation

Master the empirical evidence and primary texts required for the Summative Essay: Keynes' 1930 economic predictions, the official Finnish Government trial data, and OPVL source analysis.

📜 Source 1: John Maynard Keynes (1930)

Text: Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren.
Key Argument: Writing in the depths of the Great Depression, Keynes predicted that in 100 years (by 2030), capital accumulation and technical efficiency would increase living standards eightfold, resolving mankind's historical "economic struggle for subsistence."
The 15-Hour Workweek: He predicted people would work roughly three hours a day (15 hours/week), shifting human purpose from survival to the art of life, leisure, and philosophy.

🇫🇮 Source 2: The Finnish Basic Income Trial (Helsinki Times)

Study Details: Nationwide random trial conducted in Finland (2017–2018) by social insurance agency Kela. 2,000 unemployed individuals received €560 monthly unconditionally.
Empirical Findings:

  • Wellbeing: Statistically significant surge in life satisfaction, mental resilience, self-confidence, and trust in politicians, police, and legal courts.
  • Employment: Recipients worked an average of 6 more days per year than the control group, disproving the myth that free income induces complete lethargy.

📰 Source 3: The Economist & MIT Technology Review

The Fiscal Dilemma: Opponents point out that a universal cash payment of $12,000/year to 250 million US adults costs ~$3 trillion annually. Funding this requires aggressive VAT (value-added tax), carbon dividends, wealth taxes, or land value taxation (Henry George).
Inflation Risk: In inelastic markets (like urban rental housing with constrained supply), landlords may raise rents to capture the cash, proving UBI must be paired with public housing supply policies.

OPVL Analytical Framework for IB Criterion D
Origin: Who created the source? When? Where? What is the author's background?
Purpose: Why does this document exist? To persuade? To record empirical scientific data? To propagandize?
Value: What unique insights does it offer? Is it backed by peer-reviewed methodology or primary eyewitness experience?
Limitation: What does the source omit? What biases, national contexts, or funding conflicts must we take into account?

Devon's Case in One Sentence

A basic income floor gives people the security to make better decisions — and the real-world evidence shows they keep working, spend it on essentials, and end up healthier, happier, and more employable, not less.

Step 1 — Define It Before They Do

Whoever defines the motion controls the debate. Define UBI in your first thirty seconds, and define a modest, funded version. If you let the opposition define it, they will argue against $3,000 a month for everyone funded by printing money, and you will spend the entire round defending something you never proposed.

  • Universal: Every adult citizen receives it, regardless of existing wealth or income.
  • Unconditional: Zero means-testing, no job search logs, and no caseworker approvals.
  • Individual: Paid directly to the individual person, not per household.

Step 2 — Your Three Core Arguments

1. Security Breeds Initiative, Not Laziness

In Stockton, California, people given $500/month saw full-time employment surge from 28% to 40%. The ability to cover an unexpected $400 cash emergency doubled. When people have security, they take the risk to attend job interviews, retrain, and pursue higher-skilled work.

The opposition will claim people stop working. In Finland, unemployed recipients worked 6 days more and suffered far less depression.

Attack 1“People will just stop working and sit on the sofa.”▶
The data from every real-world pilot shows this does not happen. In Dauphin, primary earners dropped hours by just 1%. In Finland, recipients worked more than the control group. Attack 2“It is unaffordable. The numbers do not add up.”▶
Gross cost is not net cost. Wealthier citizens pay their basic income straight back in progressive taxes. Attack 3“It will cause inflation — landlords will just swallow the money.”▶

Attack 4“Why give money to millionaires? Elon Musk doesn't need $1,000.”▶
Universality eliminates the humiliating stigma of welfare and destroys the bureaucratic apparatus required to police eligibility. The rich receive the check, but through progressive income and wealth taxes, they pay it back hundreds of times over.

Attack 5“People will spend it on alcohol, drugs, and gambling.”▶

Attack 6“Work gives life meaning. UBI destroys purpose.”▶
UBI does not ban work. It empowers people to engage in vital unpaid work that our current GDP model ignores: raising children, caring for elderly grandparents, volunteering, and artistic creation.
The Turnaround: “If the opposition believes work gives meaning, why do they only count work when a corporate boss pays you a wage? Why does caring for a sick parent not count?”
Attack 7“It will trigger a border crisis and attract mass immigration.”▶
Eligibility rules are defined by policy design. Just like state pensions, national healthcare, and voting rights, UBI is distributed to legal citizens and long-term permanent residents with established residency criteria.
Attack 8“The OpenResearch study showed a 1–2 hour weekly drop in work!”▶
This is their best card—concede it and turn it into a victory! The 3-year US OpenResearch trial did find hours worked dropped by 1.3 hours/week. But look at where the time went: into community education, parenting, and seeking higher-wage careers!
The Turnaround: “If a single mother works one hour less a week so she can read bedtime stories to her child, that is not an economic loss; that is the very definition of a civilized society.”

Historical & Empirical Compendium

Universal Basic Income: 500-Year Timeline, Ideological Roots & Empirical Studies

To score at the highest band (7–8) in IB Criterion D, students must ground their arguments in deep historical origins, comparative economic philosophies, and empirical study metrics.

🏛 1. The 500-Year Intellectual Timeline

1516 — Thomas More (Utopia)

English philosopher Sir Thomas More noted that executing starving thieves for stealing bread was both cruel and irrational: "No penalty on earth will stop people from stealing, if it's their only way of getting food... It would be far more to the point to provide everyone with some means of livelihood." His friend Johannes Ludovicus Vives (1526) drafted the first practical municipal welfare plan in Bruges.

1797 — Thomas Paine (Agrarian Justice)

Revolutionary philosopher Thomas Paine argued that the Earth, in its natural uncultivated state, was the common property of all humanity. Landowners who privatized land owed society a "ground rent". Paine proposed creating a national fund to pay £15 to every citizen reaching age 21, and £10 annually to everyone aged 50+, as an unconditional universal birthright.

1962 — Milton Friedman (Negative Income Tax)

Nobel laureate and free-market icon Milton Friedman advocated the Negative Income Tax (NIT) in Capitalism and Freedom. If an individual's income fell below the tax threshold, the government sent them cash equal to a percentage of the shortfall. Friedman wanted to abolish bloated, paternalistic government welfare agencies and let the free market price mechanism allocate goods.

1967 — Martin Luther King Jr. (Guaranteed Income)

In his final book, Where Do We Go From Here: Chaos or Community?, MLK concluded that civil rights legislation alone could never cure systemic poverty without direct economic rights: "The simplest approach will prove to be the most effective — the solution to poverty is to abolish it directly by a now widely discussed measure: the guaranteed income."

1969 — The 1,000 Economists' Petition & Nixon's FAP

Over 1,000 prominent economists (including Nobel laureates Paul Samuelson, James Tobin, and John Kenneth Galbraith) urged Congress to enact a guaranteed income. President Richard Nixon introduced the Family Assistance Plan (FAP) guaranteeing $1,600/yr for a family of 4. It passed the US House twice (243 to 155), but died in the Senate over partisan disputes and marital breakup myths.

1986 — Foundation of BIEN (Basic Income Earth Network)

Formed by European philosophers and economists Philippe Van Parijs and Guy Standing, BIEN codified the formal global definition of UBI: a periodic cash payment unconditionally delivered to all on an individual basis, without means-test or work requirement.

🧭 3. The Ideological Spectrum: Why Left, Right & Center Back UBI

The Progressive / Left-Wing Case

Key Thinkers: Guy Standing, Karl Marx, David Graeber.
Arguments: De-commodification of human labor; grants workers the unconditional leverage to walk away from hazardous, humiliating, or underpaid jobs; compensates vital unpaid labor (domestic care, parenting, community work); ends poverty immediately as a basic human right.

The Free-Market / Libertarian Case

Key Thinkers: Milton Friedman, Friedrich Hayek, Charles Murray.
Arguments: Completely eliminates paternalistic, bloated government bureaucracies and intrusive caseworker surveillance; replaces complex minimum wage laws and rent control; trusts individuals as rational consumers to allocate resources in a free market.

The Civil Rights & Humanist Case

Key Thinkers: Martin Luther King Jr., Thomas Paine, Philippe Van Parijs.
Arguments: Real freedom is impossible without economic freedom. Universal access to the common inheritance of natural resources (land, minerals, technology accumulated by past generations) belongs to every human child at birth.

💰 4. Cost Math & The "Transfer Illusion" (Gross vs. Net Cost)

Opponents frequently cite the Gross Cost to shock audiences: "Giving $1,000/month to 250 million US adults costs $3 trillion every year — that is impossible!"
Economists call this the Transfer Illusion. Here is why the math works:

  • Net Cost vs Gross Cost: In a universal program funded by progressive taxation, higher earners receive their $1,000 check, but pay back $1,000 (plus additional taxes) on their income. The money is not consumed or destroyed; it is a transfer. The actual net cost (the net transfer to low-income households) is approximately $500–$600 billion, less than 3% of US GDP.
  • Offsetting Existing Spending: A clean UBI absorbs dozens of overlapping, expensive, means-tested cash assistance programs, saving hundreds of billions in administrative caseworker overhead.
  • Savings on Collateral Poverty Costs: Poverty costs the US economy over $1 trillion annually in emergency healthcare visits, crime enforcement, incarceration, and lost cognitive tax. Eradicating extreme poverty generates massive fiscal savings across all public budgets.
  • Funding Pillars: Carbon Fee & Dividend (taxing polluters and returning 100% of revenue to citizens), Land Value Tax (untaxing wages and taxing unimproved land values), and Value-Added Taxes (VAT) on automated corporate outputs.
