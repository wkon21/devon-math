How this guide works

Every checklist is collected again in the cheat sheet at the bottom. Type an answer and press Check, or open Show solution for the steps. Numerical answers can be typed with or without the unit — 15 and 15 m/s both pass — and m/s² or ms^-2 are both accepted where a unit is the answer. Your class takes g = 10 N/kg (the real value is 9.81), so every number here uses 10. Progress saves automatically in this browser.

Exam & lab toolkit

Criterion A, B and C — and the Hooke's Law investigation, step by step

Criterion A — the quiz

Knowing and understanding. It is closed-book and mostly calculation.

  • Write the formula first, then substitute, then solve. Marks are given for the substitution even if the arithmetic slips.
  • Convert before you calculate: grams → kg, cm → m, minutes → s, km/h → m/s (÷ 3.6).
  • Every answer gets a unit. A bare number is an incomplete answer.
  • Learn the three triangles: v = s/t, a = Δv/t, F = ma. Cover the one you want.
  • Direction is part of a vector answer — say “east”, “upward” or give the sign.

Criterion B — inquiring and designing

This is the written design: question, hypothesis, variables, method.

How does X affect Y?
If the IV increases, then the DV will increase / decrease, because …
  • IV = what you change. DV = what you measure. CVs = everything you keep the same.
  • Give the IV its actual values in the question: “(1 N, 2 N, 3 N, 4 N, 5 N)”.
  • Never write the word amount. Use mass, length, force, time, volume, height, number of…
  • The hypothesis needs scientific reasoning, not just a prediction.
  • Method must be repeatable by a stranger: numbered steps, named apparatus, stated repeats.

Criterion C — processing and evaluating

  • Data table needs a title, the names of the IV and DV, and units in the column heading only, in brackets.
  • Same number of decimal places all the way down a column.
  • At least 3 trials, then an average column.
  • Graph the IV on the x-axis, the DV on the y-axis, and plot the averages only.
  • Include a sample calculation — one worked line showing how an average or a gradient was found.
  • Conclusion answers the question with data quoted. Evaluation names real weaknesses and a specific improvement each.

The named lab — Hooke's Law (Summative Criterion B and C)

You investigate the relationship between the force applied to an elastic material and its extension. A spring is the elastic material; hanging masses supply the force.

  • Research question: How does the force applied to a spring (1.0 N, 2.0 N, 3.0 N, 4.0 N, 5.0 N) affect the extension of the spring?
  • IV: force applied, in N. Remember W = m × g, so a 100 g mass is 0.1 kg and supplies 1 N.
  • DV: extension of the spring, in cm or m. Extension is stretched length − original length — never the stretched length itself. This is the single most common mistake in this lab.
  • CVs: the same spring throughout, the same clamp and ruler position, the ruler vertical and read at eye level, masses added one at a time, the spring allowed to settle before reading.
  • Hypothesis: If the force applied to the spring increases, then the extension will increase in direct proportion, because each equal increment of force stretches the spring by the same amount until the limit of proportionality is reached.
  • Apparatus: clamp stand and boss, spring, 100 g slotted mass hanger and masses, 30 cm ruler, safety goggles, a mat or tray under the masses.

  • Table title: “The effect of the force applied on the extension of a spring”. Units in the headings only, one decimal place throughout.
  • Graph: force on the x-axis (it is the IV), extension on the y-axis. The points should lie on a straight line through the origin.
  • Sample calculation: average = (7.1 + 6.9 + 7.0) ÷ 3 = 7.0 cm; extension = 7.0 − 5.0 = 2.0 cm.
  • Finding k: plotted the class way, the gradient is extension ÷ force, which is 1/k. For the row above, 0.020 m ÷ 1.0 N = 0.020 m/N, so k = 1 ÷ 0.020 = 50 N/m. (Plot force on the y-axis instead and the gradient is k — say which you did.)
  • Evaluation points worth writing: reading the ruler at an angle (parallax) → clamp the ruler and read at eye level; the spring swinging → let it settle; loading past the limit of proportionality permanently deforms the spring → keep the load small and check the spring returns to 5.0 cm between runs.
