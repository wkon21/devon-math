Part 1: Key science concepts

1aWhat is a mutation? (2 marks)

Model answer

A mutation is a change in the sequence of DNA (Krasileva et al. 4). Mutations happen naturally when DNA is copied or repaired and a rare error occurs; they can have negative effects, but they also let organisms differ from each other and evolve new traits (Krasileva et al. 2). Scientists can also cause an induced mutation using a chemical, as this study did (Krasileva et al. 2).

Science terms used: mutation, DNA, evolve, traits, induced mutation

1bWhat is a polyploid organism, and why do mutations in polyploids often stay hidden? (3 marks)

Model answer

A polyploid organism has more than two copies of every gene; for example tetraploid wheat has four copies and hexaploid wheat has six (Krasileva et al. 1). Humans, by contrast, are diploid, with one copy, or allele, from each parent (Krasileva et al. 1).

Mutations stay hidden because the other, unmutated copies of the gene cover up the effect, so the plant carries the mutation but its phenotype looks the same as the wild variety (Krasileva et al. 1).

Science terms used: polyploid, gene, tetraploid, hexaploid, diploid, allele, phenotype

Part 2: Positives

2Discuss the overall positives of this wheat mutation research. (6 marks)

Model answer

Food security. Wheat supplies about 20% of the world's calories and protein (Krasileva et al. 1), so better varieties could raise yield and improve food security for a hungry world (Krasileva et al. 4).

Nutrition. Breeders can use the mutations to increase the minerals or protein in each plant (Krasileva et al. 4).

Environment and climate. The mutations could help breed wheat with traits that adapt to a changing environment, which matters more as climate change continues (Krasileva et al. 4).

A shared resource for science. The team catalogued over 10 million mutations in the wheat genome, which researchers and breeders can use to study what genes do (Krasileva et al. 1). Tetraploid plants are especially useful because their fewer gene copies make visible mutants easier to produce (Krasileva et al. 4).

Cost and reliability. Using enzymes and a capture-array to sequence only the protein-coding genes avoided the high cost of whole genome sequencing (Krasileva et al. 2). Comparing against control plants gave a low error rate with few "fake" mutations (Krasileva et al. 3).

Science terms used: protein, yield, food security, traits, genome, tetraploid, enzymes, capture-array, whole genome sequencing, control

Part 3: Negatives and limitations

3Discuss the overall negatives or limitations of this wheat mutation research. (6 marks)

Model answer

Slow for bread wheat. Bread wheat is hexaploid, so breeders may need to combine mutations in all six copies to reveal an effect, which takes longer than working with tetraploid wheat (Krasileva et al. 4).

Single mutations do little. Individual mutations in starch synthesis or flowering genes had a negligible effect compared with plants mutated in every copy of the gene (Krasileva et al. 4), so the database does not deliver instant improvements.

Harmful effects are possible. Mutations can have negative effects (Krasileva et al. 2); one mutant phenotype produced no flowers at all, so it could not make grain (Krasileva et al. 2).

Incomplete picture. Only protein-coding regions were captured for DNA sequencing (Krasileva et al. 2), so mutations elsewhere in the genome were not studied. Even the original plants carried mutations compared with the reference genome (Krasileva et al. 3), which complicates comparisons.

Social and ethical concerns (your own evaluation). The mutations were induced with a chemical (Krasileva et al. 2). Some consumers may distrust "mutant" crops, and the article itself notes that people often think of mutants as monstrous or strange (Krasileva et al. 2). Farmers may also need to buy new seed, which raises economic questions for poorer regions.

Science terms used: hexaploid, tetraploid, starch synthesis, phenotype, DNA sequencing, genome, induced mutation

When a point goes beyond the article, say so. Examiners reward clear reasoning, but a citation should only back up what the article actually states.

Part 4: Relevance

4Is this article relevant? Justify your answer. (4 marks)

Model answer

Yes, the article is relevant. It is an adaptation of a peer-reviewed study published in PNAS in 2017 (Krasileva et al. 5), so the science is reliable, and it names the original researchers and editors (Krasileva et al. 1).

The topic still matters: wheat is one of the most widely grown crops and a major source of calories and protein (Krasileva et al. 1), and crops that cope with climate change are increasingly needed for food security (Krasileva et al. 4).

However, it has limits as a source. It was written for students, so it simplifies the method, and the original research is several years old. For a full evaluation you would check newer studies on wheat breeding.

Science terms used: peer-reviewed, protein, food security
